# Explain and Present

People can hold only about three to four new, interacting ideas in mind at
once. What they already know raises that limit, because familiar material is
held as a single chunk. Every explanation spends this small budget on three
things:

- the subject itself: how many new elements must be understood together,
  relative to what the audience already knows;
- the presentation: searching, cross-referencing, decoding jargon, and
  filtering material that does not matter;
- the audience's own work of connecting the new idea to what they know.

Remove the presentation cost, deliver the subject in pieces the audience can
hold, and leave room for the connecting work. Clarity depends on the audience:
guidance that helps a newcomer slows an expert down.

For the research behind each rule, its strength, and where it stops applying,
read [references/evidence.md](references/evidence.md).

## 1. Fix the audience, goal, and message

- **Audience.** Establish who receives it, what they already know, the words
  they use, how much time and attention they have, and how they feel about the
  subject. Prefer evidence to imagination: their earlier messages, their role,
  one quick question. Only when their level is unknown, give them a way to
  adjust it, such as "If you already know X, skip to Y."
- **Goal.** Name what they must be able to do afterwards, then choose the mode:
  - *Brief*: they decide or act now. Remove every effort that does not serve
    the decision.
  - *Teach*: they must do or explain it later. Remove wasted effort, then add a
    little useful effort, such as predicting, explaining, or applying, because
    effortful recall builds lasting understanding. Give newcomers worked
    examples before asking them to work alone.
  - *Align*: they must change or share a view. Start from common ground, then
    give the evidence.

  In every mode, include what reaching the outcome requires. When they must
  carry something out, that includes the mistakes most likely to defeat it, a
  way to confirm that it worked, and any existing tool that already does part
  of the job.
- **Message.** Write the main message in one to three plain sentences before
  drafting anything else. If you cannot, finish the thinking first; structure
  will not hide an unclear point.
- **Accuracy.** Simplify how you say it, not what is true. Add no confidence,
  cause, or detail the evidence does not support, and label estimates and
  assumptions as such. Examples and code they may copy must be correct for
  every case they claim to handle and agree with the surrounding text.
- **Medium.** A reader sets the pace and can reread. A listener cannot, because
  speech disappears. Slides with speech split attention between two sources.
  Section 5 adapts the plan to each.

For outlines of common jobs, such as a decision brief, a concept explanation,
an incident report, a status update, or a talk, read
[references/structures.md](references/structures.md).

## 2. Lead with what matters to them

- Open with the answer, decision, or request, and why it matters to this
  audience. Busy readers judge from the first lines whether to continue and
  how much effort the rest will take.
- Make the one action obvious: state it once, early, with a single path. Extra
  requests compete for the same attention, so the main one is easier to miss.
- Give the support in descending importance. Put detail after it, clearly
  labeled, so the audience can stop once they have enough. Cut by relevance to
  the goal rather than by length, and never hide what they need to know.
- Change the order when the conclusion means nothing without setup (give a
  one-line map of where you are going instead), when the audience must judge
  the argument before the claim, when the reader is hostile (one sentence of
  shared ground first), or when a required format fixes the order.
- For anything longer than a few paragraphs or a minute of speech, announce its
  shape first: "Three parts: what changed, why, and what you need to do."

## 3. Deliver the subject in pieces they can hold

- **Count the new elements**: terms, actors, components, states, and steps.
  When more than three or four must be understood together, split the
  explanation into stages.
- **Parts before interactions.** Name each new part and say what it is, then
  show how the parts work together. When the whole would overwhelm, teach the
  pieces separately first.
- **Known to new.** Start from something the audience already knows and add one
  new idea at a time. In each sentence, put the familiar first and the new or
  important point last, and keep the subject close to its verb.
- **Concrete, then general.** Give a specific case, then name the principle it
  shows. To help people apply the idea elsewhere, give two contrasting cases
  and say what they share. An abstract statement alone gives newcomers nothing
  to attach it to.
- **Analogies** transfer structure. Say what corresponds to what and where the
  analogy stops working, because people also carry over surface features that
  do not apply.
- **Worked examples for newcomers.** Label each step by its purpose ("find the
  boundary", "check the edge case"). Hand more of the steps to the audience as
  they show skill.
- **Match depth to expertise.** With experts, skip what they know, use their
  precise terms, and give the dense version. Over-explaining to an expert costs
  less than under-explaining to a newcomer, so when unsure, start a little
  simpler and offer a faster path.

## 4. Remove presentation cost

- **Cut what does not serve the goal**: interesting asides, history, tangents,
  stacked hedges, and decorative images. An anecdote that illustrates the actual
  point is not an aside.
- **Keep together what must be read together.** Label the diagram itself instead
  of using a legend, put the explanation beside the code or number it explains,
  and avoid "see above" or "as mentioned earlier".
- **Do not say the same thing twice in parallel.** Do not read slide text aloud
  word for word or restate the adjacent table in a paragraph. Short key terms
  beside a visual are fine.
- **Signal the structure and the one important thing.** Use headings that state
  the point and opening sentences that say what each paragraph does. Reserve
  emphasis for what must not be missed; emphasis on everything is emphasis on
  nothing.
- **One name per thing.** Choose a term and keep it. Switching synonyms makes the
  audience wonder whether there are two things.
- **Use their words.** Replace jargon, acronyms, and internal names with words
  the audience uses. Defining jargon does not fully restore attention, so keep a
  technical term only when it is unavoidable or they will need it again, and
  define it once, where it first appears.
- **Write plain sentences**: everyday words, active voice by default (passive
  when the actor is unknown or irrelevant), and verbs instead of nouns built
  from verbs. Treat a sentence over about 25 words as a prompt to check it, not
  a rule. Chopping apart sentences that need "because", "unless", or "so" makes
  reasoning harder.
- **Choose the form by the shape of the content**:
  - prose for reasons, trade-offs, and causal chains, because connecting words
    carry the logic;
  - bullets for parallel, independent items, and numbered steps when order
    matters;
  - a table when several items are compared on the same attributes;
  - a diagram for spatial, structural, temporal, or causal relations, with one
    idea per diagram;
  - a sentence, not a list, for one or two items.

## 5. Adapt to the medium

Before producing slides, a talk, a chart, a diagram, or numbers about risk or
probability, read [references/media.md](references/media.md). In short:

- **Written**: the answer and the request first, headings whose first words
  carry the meaning, short paragraphs, and detail later.
- **Spoken**: keep segments short, say the structure, mark each transition,
  recap before each new step, and keep the key term or diagram visible while
  you discuss it. Break up a long talk by changing activity, such as a question
  or an example, rather than by talking longer.
- **Slides**: a full-sentence headline that states the claim, visual evidence
  beneath it, and detail in speech or a handout.
- **Charts**: one message stated in the title, the important comparison made the
  easiest one to see, the key series highlighted with the rest muted, and labels
  placed on the data.
- **Numbers**: absolute figures with a reference group and time frame, counts
  ("8 out of 100 people") for conditional or risk questions, and never a
  relative change on its own.

## 6. Check understanding

Neither your sense that it was clear nor the audience's nods are reliable
evidence. Experts underestimate what newcomers lack, people overrate their own
understanding until they try to explain, and a smooth explanation can feel
understood without being understood. Audiences can even prefer formats they
learn less from.

- **Live**: after each chunk, ask the audience to put it in their own words or
  apply it, framed as a check on your explanation: "So I know I explained it
  well, how would you handle this case?" Ask "What questions do you have?"
  rather than "Any questions?". Ask them to predict what happens next or to
  apply the idea to a variation.
- **Written or delayed**: reread the draft as someone arriving cold. Confirm
  that the first lines alone say what this is and what is needed; that every
  term, acronym, internal name, and skipped step is known to this audience or
  explained; that every claim is one you can support; and that every list,
  heading, and visual earns its place. When the
  stakes justify it, ask someone without context to read it and say what they
  would do next.
- **When a check fails**, explain differently: a new example, another
  representation, or a smaller step. Repeating the same explanation longer or
  louder does not help.
- In a brief, keep the check to an easy way to ask for more. Do not quiz a
  decision-maker.

## Avoid

- Writing to a readability score. Formulas count word and sentence length; they
  do not show whether readers can find, understand, or use the content.
- Treating "seven plus or minus two" as a limit on bullets or menu items. The
  meaningful limit applies to new ideas held together, and it is closer to four.
- Tailoring content to "learning styles", or repeating claims such as "people
  remember 10% of what they read", "93% of communication is nonverbal", or a
  ten-minute attention span. None holds up.
- Applying any rule here everywhere. Each has conditions; when rules conflict,
  the audience's knowledge and the goal decide.
