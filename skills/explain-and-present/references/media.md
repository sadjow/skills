# Adapting to the medium

## Written documents and messages

- Readers scan before they read. Put the answer and any request in the first
  lines, and start headings, links, and list items with the words that carry
  their meaning. Readers often see only the first few words.
- Use headings that say what the section concludes or contains, so the
  headings alone tell the story.
- Keep one idea per paragraph and open each paragraph with its point.
- Put optional detail after the main content, behind a clear label or a link,
  so readers who have enough can stop. Do not hide anything they need to act.
- Shorter messages get more responses. Cut words that do not change what the
  reader understands or does, but keep the reasoning a reader needs to trust the
  conclusion.

## Spoken explanations and talks

Speech is transient: the listener cannot reread a sentence, so working memory
must hold what was just said while processing what comes next.

- Keep segments short and finish one idea before starting the next.
- Say the structure at the start, mark each transition ("That was the cause.
  Now the fix."), and recap before building on a previous point.
- Keep the key term, number, or diagram visible on screen while you talk about
  it, so the audience does not have to remember it.
- Narrate a visual rather than putting the same words on screen and reading
  them. A few key terms on screen help; full sentences read aloud do not.
- Attention does not drop on a fixed timer, but it wanders during long
  uninterrupted listening. Short questions, predictions, or exercises between
  segments keep people engaged and show you what landed.
- Repetition is useful in speech in a way it is not in writing: restate the
  main message at the start, after each part, and at the end.

## Slides

- Make each headline a full-sentence claim, such as "Caching cut page load time
  by half", not a topic label such as "Caching results".
- Support the claim with visual evidence: a chart, diagram, photo, or code
  fragment. Avoid bullet lists of sentences.
- Put the explanation in your speech, and the detail in speaker notes or a
  handout.
- One message per slide. When a slide needs a second claim, it needs a second
  slide.
- Audiences often say they prefer text-heavy slides even when they learn less
  from them, so their preference is not a reliable test.

## Charts

- Decide the one comparison the chart must make obvious, then choose the
  encoding. People judge position along a common scale most accurately, then
  length, then angle and area, and colour intensity least. Use bars or dots on
  a shared axis for precise comparison.
- State the message in the title: "Sign-ups doubled after the redesign", not
  "Sign-ups by month".
- Highlight the series or values that carry the message and mute the rest in
  grey. Remove gridlines, borders, and decoration that do not help reading.
- Label lines and bars directly instead of using a legend, so the reader does
  not have to look back and forth.
- Place the items to be compared next to each other and on the same scale.
- Many readers have limited graph literacy. When the audience is broad, add a
  sentence that says what the chart shows.
- Memorable is not the same as understood. Decoration can make a chart
  memorable without making it clearer.

## Diagrams

- Use a diagram when the content has spatial, structural, temporal, or causal
  relations. A good diagram places related information together, so the reader
  sees the relation instead of searching for it.
- Show one idea per diagram. When it needs more, build it up in stages across
  several diagrams.
- Label elements on the diagram itself, and keep the text that explains a part
  next to that part.
- Use consistent shapes, colours, and arrow meanings, and say what they mean
  when it is not obvious.
- A diagram does not replace explanation for newcomers. Walk them through it in
  the order the parts interact.

## Numbers, risk, and probability

- Give absolute numbers with a reference group and a time frame: "3 out of 1,000
  people over 10 years", not "a 50% increase". A relative change on its own
  exaggerates small risks.
- When comparing risks, keep the same denominator ("8 out of 100" versus "4 out
  of 100"), and avoid "1 in x" formats, which are harder to compare.
- For conditional questions, such as how likely a positive test means the
  condition is present, present natural frequencies: "Of 1,000 people, 10 have
  the condition. 9 of them test positive. Of the other 990, 89 also test
  positive." People reason correctly far more often with counts than with
  percentages and conditional probabilities.
- Do not make the audience calculate. Give the result they need.
- An icon array, a grid of 100 or 1,000 figures with the affected ones
  highlighted, helps people with limited numeracy see proportions.
- Round to the precision the decision needs, and say how certain the number is.

## Stories and narrative

- A short story or case raises attention and makes an abstract point concrete.
  Tie it to the point explicitly.
- Narratives persuade as well as inform, often without the audience noticing.
  When the goal is to inform a decision, present the evidence alongside the
  story and do not let a single vivid case stand in for the overall numbers.
