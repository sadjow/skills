# Evidence behind the guidance

Checked on 2026-10-29. Strength reflects the kind of evidence, not how often a
claim is repeated: **strong** means meta-analyses or replicated experiments,
**moderate** means several experiments or an expert practice guide, **limited**
means few or indirect studies, and **practice** means professional consensus or
a standard without controlled tests.

| Working memory holds about 3 to 5 chunks of new information; prior knowledge makes chunks larger. | Count new interacting elements; split above three or four. | Strong | Applies to new, interacting elements, not to items in a list the reader can scan. | Cowan 2001; Cowan 2015 |
| Load comes from the subject's complexity relative to the learner, from how it is presented, and from the learner's integration work. | Remove presentation cost, sequence the subject, leave room for connecting work. | Moderate | Load types are hard to measure separately, so the framework guides design better than it predicts outcomes. | Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; de Jong 2010 |

## Myths to avoid

| Claim | Status | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matching teaching to a learner's preferred "learning style" improves learning. | Not supported by studies that test the matching directly. | Pashler et al. 2008 |
| People remember 10% of what they read and 90% of what they do. | The percentages have no research basis; they were added to Dale's cone of experience later. | Subramony et al. 2014 |
| 93% of communication is nonverbal (7-38-55). | Mehrabian's studies concerned only feelings and attitudes in inconsistent messages, and he rejects the general claim. | Mehrabian, author's site |
| Attention drops after 10 minutes. | Not supported; attention varies with the activity and lapses throughout. | Wilson & Korn 2007; Bradbury 2016 |
| Working memory holds seven plus or minus two items, so lists should be short. | The meaningful limit is about four new chunks; it does not apply to items readers can scan and revisit. | Cowan 2001 |
| Human attention spans are shorter than a goldfish's. | Traces to a marketing report without supporting data. | Microsoft Canada consumer insights report, 2015 |

## Where the guidance conflicts

- **Removing effort versus adding productive difficulty.** Reducing load helps
  people understand now; effortful retrieval, explanation, and spacing help them
  remember and transfer later. The skill resolves this by goal: in a brief,
  remove all unnecessary effort; when teaching, remove waste first, then add
  effort that makes people use the idea.
- **Brevity versus completeness.** Shorter messages get more action, but cutting
  the reasoning can lower trust or hide what readers need. Cut by relevance to
  the goal, and move optional detail later rather than removing it.
- **Answer first versus building up.** Answer first suits busy readers and
  decisions. Setup first suits conclusions that are meaningless without
  context, hostile audiences, and arguments the audience must evaluate. Even
  then, give a one-line map of where the explanation is going.
- **Short sentences versus connected reasoning.** Short sentences lower load,
  but splitting causal reasoning into fragments removes the connecting words
  that carry it.
- **Multimedia effect sizes.** Many design effects are smaller in independent
  and later studies than in early ones from the original laboratories, and they
  vary with learner knowledge, pacing, and content (Cromley & Chen 2025). Treat
  the principles as defaults to check, not guarantees.

## Access limits

Several sources were checked through abstracts, publisher summaries, or
secondary reviews rather than full text: the 2025 expertise reversal
meta-analysis, Mayer 2024, Cromley & Chen 2025, Seddon et al. 2021, and the
seductive details effect size. Effect sizes quoted above come from sources whose
results were confirmed directly; others are described without a number.
