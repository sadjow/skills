# Structures for common jobs

Each outline puts what the audience needs first. Drop any part that does not
serve the goal, and keep the order unless one of the reasons in section 2 of the
skill applies.

## Decision brief

For someone who must choose, approve, or schedule something.

1. The decision needed and its deadline, with dates and times in the reader's
   time zone.
2. Your recommendation and the main reason for it.
3. The options, compared in a table on the attributes the decision-maker cares
   about, such as cost, risk, time, and who is affected.
4. Risks and unknowns, with what would change the recommendation.
5. Supporting detail, labeled as optional.

Example opening: "I need your choice of maintenance window by Thursday. I
recommend Sunday 02:00 to 04:00, because it affects about 40 customers instead of
900."

## Concept explanation

For someone who must understand and later use an idea.

1. One sentence in the audience's terms: what it is and which problem it solves
   for them.
2. A concrete case they recognize.
3. The parts: name each new element and say what it is.
4. How the parts interact, step by step, with the purpose of each step.
5. The general principle, a contrasting case that shows its boundary, and where
   any analogy you used stops working.
6. A check: ask them to predict an outcome or apply the idea to a variation.
7. A summary of one to three sentences.

## Change or incident report

For people affected by something that happened or is about to happen.

1. What happened and its effect now.
2. What the reader must do, if anything, and by when.
3. The cause in one sentence.
4. A short timeline, only when the sequence matters to the reader.
5. The fix and how it was verified.
6. What is still open and when the next update will arrive.

## Status update

For people tracking progress.

1. The current state compared with the plan: on track, at risk, or late.
2. What you need from the reader, if anything.
3. Progress since the last update.
4. Risks, each with its effect and what is being done.
5. Next steps and the date of the next update.

## Talk or presentation

For a live audience that cannot reread.

1. Write the one message the audience should leave with.
2. Divide the talk into three parts or fewer, and tell the audience what they are.
3. For each slide or segment, write a full-sentence claim as the headline and
   choose the visual evidence that supports it.
4. After each part, recap it in one sentence and check understanding with a
   question or prediction before moving on.
5. Close by restating the message and the one action you want.
